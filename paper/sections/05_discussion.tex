\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The results in Section~\ref{sec:results} answer the four questions posed
in Section~\ref{sec:introduction}, but several patterns recur across them
that are worth drawing out explicitly.

\subsection{Simplicity Outperforms Sophistication}
\label{sec:discussion-simplicity}

\texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} is the simplest correction audited in this
study. It moves the decision boundary from 0.5 to the class prior and does
nothing else (Section~\ref{sec:methods-corrections}). It is also the most
robust method tested by nearly every measure, with the lowest CHR and the
highest mean CG in both directions (Section~\ref{sec:results-rq1}) and the
only TabPFN correction whose calibration never falls below its own
baseline's (Section~\ref{sec:results-reliability}). The one exception is
break-even share, where several XGBoost corrections actually stay
net-beneficial through the full contamination range more often than
\texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} does, particularly under Direction B
(Section~\ref{sec:results-rq3}), which is the same divide between failure
frequency and failure severity that separates \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample}
from \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} in Section~\ref{sec:results-rq2}.
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t}, the two
corrections that rescale TabPFN's predictions against both a prior and a
temperature term (Section~\ref{sec:methods-corrections}), have the lowest
mean CG and the highest regret of any method in both directions.

This finding builds on prior work rather than standing alone. Elor and
Averbuch-Elor~\cite{elor2022smote} found that on clean, uncorrupted data, a
well-tuned classifier often gains nothing from balancing the training set,
and can lose performance from it. This study's contribution is to show
that the same pattern holds, and grows sharper, once label noise is added.
A correction is not just sometimes unnecessary. It can actively backfire,
and in this comparison the two most mathematically elaborate corrections,
DistPFN and DistPFN-T, are the ones that backfire hardest, not the
simplest one. A practitioner choosing a correction method for a TabPFN
deployment under uncertain label quality has, based on this study's
results, no clear reason to prefer a more elaborate posterior-rescaling
method over a simple threshold shift.

\subsection{Direction Asymmetry Undercuts a Single Robustness Score}
\label{sec:discussion-direction}

Section~\ref{sec:results-rq2} found that five of seven methods fail more
often under Direction A (majority rows relabeled as minority) than
Direction B (minority rows relabeled as majority), while the two DistPFN
variants show the opposite pattern. This asymmetry is exactly what the
label-noise literature's class-conditional and asymmetric-noise
formalizations predict in principle~\cite{natarajan2013noisylabels,scott2013asymmetricnoise},
but this study's results show it is not a minor theoretical caveat. A
method's CHR under one direction tells you little about its CHR under the
other. \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample}'s CHR is 31.7\% under Direction A and
7.5\% under Direction B, a more than fourfold difference in the same
method on the same datasets. A robustness claim validated under only one
corruption mechanism, or under the symmetric random-flip noise more
commonly studied, does not transfer to a directed, class-conditional error
of the kind this study injects, and in practice the true direction of
label error is rarely known in advance. This is the strongest argument in
this study for testing every correction under both directions rather than
one.

\subsection{The Corrupted Prior Is Not the Main Failure Mode}
\label{sec:discussion-prior}

The most surprising result in Section~\ref{sec:results-rq4} is that mean
PSG is negative for all three prior-dependent TabPFN methods in both
directions. Handing a correction the true, uncorrupted prior, something no
real deployment could ever do, does not reliably improve it over using the
corrupted, observed prior, and at high contamination the oracle variant of
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} degrades faster
than the deployable one. If the wrong prior were the primary cause of
these methods' decline, correcting it should have helped. It does not.

One structural reason is available directly from how these corrections
work (Section~\ref{sec:methods-corrections}). \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold},
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn}, and \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} all adjust
TabPFN's output probabilities after the fact, using the class
prior~\cite{saerens2002adjusting,lee2026distpfn}. None of them touch the
training rows TabPFN actually reads as its in-context
prompt~\cite{hollmann2023tabpfn,hollmann2025tabpfnv2}. Contamination
happens before any correction is applied
(Section~\ref{sec:methods-overview}), so the mislabeled rows stay in that
prompt regardless of which prior a posterior-rescaling method is given
afterward. Swapping in the true prior fixes the summary statistic the
correction uses, but not the individual wrong examples TabPFN has already
been shown. This would explain why the oracle prior helps so little,
though confirming it directly would require an experiment this study did
not run, comparing a prior-only fix against a fix that also removes the
mislabeled rows from the context itself.

\subsection{Accuracy and Calibration Answer Different Questions}
\label{sec:discussion-calibration}

Section~\ref{sec:results-reliability} found that every XGBoost correction
improves calibration over its baseline at every noise level tested, even
the ones that sometimes hurt balanced accuracy
(Section~\ref{sec:results-rq1}), while three of four TabPFN corrections
can make calibration worse than doing nothing under Direction A. Balanced
accuracy and calibration measure different properties of a classifier, and
a correction that helps one can be neutral or harmful on the other. This
matters for deployment. A system that only needs a correct label, such as
most classification benchmarks, cares primarily about balanced accuracy or
a related discrimination metric. A system that acts on a predicted
probability (setting a risk threshold, allocating a limited budget,
ranking cases by urgency) cares about calibration first. Reporting CHR and
mean CG alone, as most of Section~\ref{sec:results} does for the headline
comparisons, would miss that \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample} is uniquely bad
at calibration under Direction A even though its balanced accuracy profile
looks far more favorable there, the second-highest mean CG of any method
(Section~\ref{sec:results-rq1}). Evaluating a
correction method for deployment requires checking both axes separately,
not inferring one from the other.

\subsection{Implications for Imbalance Correction Under Label Noise}
\label{sec:discussion-implications}

Related work on class imbalance and label noise as a joint problem has so
far proposed new training-time algorithms, combining cleaning with
resampling~\cite{koziarski2020ccr}, active learning with noisy-label
training~\cite{khanal2024activelabel}, or class-balanced sample
selection~\cite{liu2024imbalancednoisy}. This study takes a different
approach. It asks whether the corrections practitioners are already using
need replacing at all before proposing something new to replace them
with. The answer is conditional. \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold}, the cheapest
and simplest correction tested, remains net-beneficial through the full
contamination range in half or more of the conditions tested, in both
directions (Section~\ref{sec:results-rq3}), which suggests that for at
least some deployments, auditing which correction is already in use and
switching to
a simpler one may close most of the gap that a new joint training
algorithm would otherwise be built to address. For the methods that do
fail badly, particularly \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} under high contamination, the case for the
kind of joint noise-and-imbalance-aware training these related works
propose is stronger. Which situation a practitioner is in is not knowable
without the kind of audit this study performs, which is itself an argument
for making this kind of evaluation routine rather than assuming a
published correction method is safe by default.
