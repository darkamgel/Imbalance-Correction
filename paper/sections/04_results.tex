\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

Across the five datasets, three seeds, four imbalance ratios, and five
contamination rates (the shared clean condition plus four noise levels in
each of the two directions), the full matrix produced 6{,}417 per-seed runs
over the nine methods (seven corrections plus the two uncorrected
baselines), seed-averaged into 2{,}139 method-condition rows. Of these,
63 runs failed outright, all \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample} at $r{=}0.50$
(Section~\ref{sec:methods-corrections}). 60 failed under Direction A
contamination, at every dataset, every nonzero $c$, and every seed, and 3
failed in the shared clean condition. Because the Direction A failures are
complete rather than sporadic, every $r{=}0.50$ block is entirely absent for
\texttt{tabpfn\_downsample} under Direction A, which is why the TabPFN
family's Direction A significance test below runs on 60 blocks rather than
80 (Section~\ref{sec:results-significance}). All other results below are
computed only over runs that completed.

\subsection{Overall Correction Robustness}
\label{sec:results-rq1}

Every one of the seven correction methods produced at least one stress cell
with negative Correction Gain, confirming the pattern anticipated in
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. No correction remains uniformly beneficial
once label error is present. How often this happens varies sharply by
method. Table~\ref{tab:robustness} summarizes Correction Harm Rate (CHR) and
mean Correction Gain (CG, on balanced accuracy) for every method in each
direction. \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} is the most robust method by both
measures in both directions. Its CHR is 11.9\% under Direction A and 0.6\%
under Direction B, an order of magnitude below every other correction under
Direction B, and its mean CG (0.053 and 0.076) is the highest of any method
in both directions. \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} sits at the other end, with
the lowest mean CG under Direction A (0.005, barely positive) and among the
highest CHR in both directions (23.1\% and 28.8\%).

\subsection{Direction-Dependent Failure}
\label{sec:results-rq2}

Corruption direction changes how often a correction fails, and the
direction of the effect is itself method-dependent rather than uniform.
Five of the seven methods have a higher CHR under Direction A
(majority rows relabeled as minority) than under Direction B (minority rows
relabeled as majority). These are \texttt{xgb\_ros}, \texttt{xgb\_smote},
\texttt{xgb\_weighted}, \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample}, and
\texttt{tabpfn\_threshold}, the last two by a wide margin
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). The two DistPFN variants show the opposite
pattern, failing more often under Direction B than Direction A.
Figure~\ref{fig:chr-regret} shows CHR and mean regret side by side for
every method, split by direction.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/chr_regret.png}
\caption{Correction Harm Rate (left) and mean regret (right, $\max(0,-\text{CG})$)
by method and direction, over noisy stress cells only. \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn}
has both a high CHR and the highest regret in both directions.
\texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} has the lowest of both in both directions.}
\label{fig:chr-regret}
\end{figure*}

Regret separates two methods that both have an elevated CHR under Direction
A, \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample} (31.7\%) and \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn}
(23.1\%). Despite that comparable failure frequency,
\texttt{tabpfn\_downsample}'s mean regret (0.0063) is well under
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn}'s (0.0089), and the gap widens further under
Direction B (0.0002 versus 0.0104). When \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample} fails,
it tends to fail by a small margin. \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn}'s failures are
both frequent and, on average, an order of magnitude larger.

Part of this asymmetry traces back to the uncorrected baselines themselves.
Comparing balanced accuracy at $c{=}0$ against $c{=}0.30$ for
\texttt{xgb\_none}, Direction B degrades performance by 0.083 (0.779 to
0.695) versus 0.030 for Direction A (0.779 to 0.749), roughly 2.8 times as
much. \texttt{tabpfn\_none} shows the same asymmetry, 0.055 versus 0.022.
Losing genuine minority rows (Direction B) is inherently more damaging to
an uncorrected model than diluting the minority class with mislabeled
majority rows (Direction A), which helps explain why a correction's
apparent robustness under one direction does not predict its robustness
under the other.

\subsection{Break-Even Contamination Rates}
\label{sec:results-rq3}

Table~\ref{tab:breakeven} reports, for each method and direction, the share
of the 20 (dataset, $r$) combinations (15 for \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample}
under Direction A, since its 5 combinations at $r{=}0.50$ have no completed
runs to compute a break-even point from) whose seed-averaged Correction
Gain never dropped to zero or below within the tested range, i.e., $c^*>0.30$.
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} is the least likely of the seven to stay
beneficial across the full contamination range. It remains net-beneficial
through $c{=}0.30$ in only 30\% of conditions in either direction, the
lowest share of any method. No single method leads at the high end of the
table. \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} stays beneficial through the full range
in 65\% of Direction B conditions, matching or trailing several XGBoost
corrections (\texttt{xgb\_ros}, \texttt{xgb\_smote}, and
\texttt{xgb\_weighted} all reach 70\% under Direction B) despite having by
far the lowest CHR and highest mean CG under that same direction. CHR
counts every failing cell individually, while break-even share only asks
whether a method's seed-averaged trend, aggregated within one (dataset,
$r$) combination, ever crosses zero. A method can have a low CHR and still
cross zero in many such combinations, which is exactly why
Section~\ref{sec:methods-contribution} defines CHR, Regret, and $c^*$ as
separate, complementary measures rather than collapsing robustness into one
number.

\subsection{Prior Sensitivity}
\label{sec:results-rq4}

The corrupted prior explains less of a correction's failure than expected.
On average, the deployable, observed-prior variant slightly outperforms
the oracle diagnostic rather than trailing it. Mean PSG
(Equation~\ref{eq:psg}) is negative for all three prior-dependent
methods in both directions. Under Direction A it is $-0.008$ for
\texttt{tabpfn\_threshold}, $-0.014$ for \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn}, and
$-0.015$ for \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t}, with similar magnitudes under
Direction B. This gap is not fixed, however. Restricted to $c{=}0.30$
only, mean PSG for \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} widens to $-0.060$ under
Direction A. Figure~\ref{fig:psg} shows the observed and oracle curves for
all three methods. At low contamination they stay close together, but for
the two DistPFN variants they separate sharply above $c{=}0.20$ under
Direction A, with the oracle variant degrading faster than the observed
one. \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} shows the same ordering but a much smaller
gap. In no case tested does using the true, uncorrected prior recover the
correction's clean-condition performance once contamination is severe, so
the corrupted prior is not the primary driver of these corrections' decline
at high contamination.

\subsection{Statistical Significance}
\label{sec:results-significance}

The Friedman test is significant at $p<10^{-11}$ for all four
family-direction combinations. XGBoost's Direction A test gives
$\chi^2{=}55.3$ ($n{=}80$ blocks), and its Direction B test gives
$\chi^2{=}91.6$ ($n{=}80$). TabPFN's Direction A test gives $\chi^2{=}64.3$
($n{=}60$), and its Direction B test gives $\chi^2{=}154.9$ ($n{=}80$).
Method identity therefore significantly affects balanced accuracy within
both families in both directions. Every uncorrected baseline differs
significantly from every correction in its family, in both directions
(Holm-corrected $p<0.001$ throughout), confirming that the corrections are
having a measurable effect, even though Section~\ref{sec:results-rq1} shows
that effect is not always an improvement. Not every pair of
corrections is distinguishable from each other, however.
\texttt{xgb\_weighted} and \texttt{xgb\_smote} are statistically
indistinguishable under Direction A ($p_{\text{Holm}}=0.92$), as are
\texttt{xgb\_ros} and \texttt{xgb\_smote} ($p_{\text{Holm}}=0.92$), and
\texttt{xgb\_weighted} and \texttt{xgb\_ros} under Direction B
($p_{\text{Holm}}=0.17$). Within the TabPFN family under Direction A,
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} are each
statistically indistinguishable from \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample}
($p_{\text{Holm}}=0.12$ for both). This is the only non-significant pair
within the TabPFN family in either direction. Every other TabPFN pairwise
comparison, in both directions, is significant.

\subsection{Calibration Reliability}
\label{sec:results-reliability}

Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} tracks Brier score and expected calibration
error against noise level for every method. Every XGBoost correction
improves on \texttt{xgb\_none}'s Brier score and ECE at every noise level
tested, in both directions, with no exception. The TabPFN family shows no
such uniformity. \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample} is worse-calibrated than the
uncorrected \texttt{tabpfn\_none} baseline on both metrics at every noise
level under Direction A, and \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} join it at $c \geq 0.20$ under the same
direction. This calibration backfire is almost entirely confined to
Direction A. Only one exception appears under Direction B
(\texttt{tabpfn\_downsample}'s Brier score at $c{=}0.05$, negligible in
magnitude at 0.003). \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} is the one TabPFN
correction that never exceeds its baseline's Brier score or ECE in either
direction, consistent with its standing as the most robust method on
balanced accuracy as well (Sections~\ref{sec:results-rq1}
and~\ref{sec:results-rq2}). At the lowest noise level tested ($c{=}0.05$),
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} are in fact the
best-calibrated methods of any tested, correction or baseline, in both
directions, before \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn}'s advantage reverses at higher
contamination.
