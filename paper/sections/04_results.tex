\section{Results and Discussion}
\label{sec:results}

Across the 26 datasets, ten seeds, four imbalance ratios, and five
contamination rates (the shared clean condition plus four noise levels in
each of the two directions), each combination forms one of 9{,}360 stress
cells. Nine methods (seven corrections plus the two uncorrected baselines)
are scored on every cell, giving 84{,}240 evaluations. The three
prior-dependent TabPFN corrections are additionally scored with the oracle
prior for diagnostic purposes (Section~\ref{sec:methods-corrections}),
adding 28{,}080 more, for 112{,}320 model-condition evaluations planned in
total. 112{,}319 completed, one cell,
\texttt{tabpfn\_downsample} on APS Failure (seed 19, $r{=}0.10$,
$c{=}0.05$, Direction A), failed to converge
(Section~\ref{sec:methods-implementation}) and is excluded from the
tables below rather than interpolated. All other results are computed
over the full matrix.

\subsection{Overall Correction Robustness}
\label{sec:results-rq1}

Every one of the seven correction methods produced at least one stress cell
with negative Correction Gain, confirming the pattern anticipated in
Section~\ref{sec:introduction}. No correction remains uniformly beneficial
once label error is present, and how often this happens varies sharply
by method. Table~\ref{tab:robustness} summarizes Correction Harm Rate (CHR) and
mean Correction Gain (CG, on balanced accuracy) for every method in each
direction. \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} is the most robust method by both
measures in both directions. Its CHR is 4.8\% under Direction A and 2.4\%
under Direction B, less than half the next-lowest correction under either
direction, and its mean CG (0.072 and 0.093) is the highest of any method
in both directions. The worst end of the table looks different by
direction, but in both directions it is the same two methods:
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} hold the highest
CHR under Direction A (35.1\% and 33.9\%) and, by a wider margin, under
Direction B (43.8\% and 47.4\%, more than five times the next-highest
method there). Pooling across all 26 datasets separates the DistPFN pair
clearly from the rest of the field in both directions, not just one.

\subsection{Simplicity Outperforms Sophistication}
\label{sec:discussion-simplicity}

\texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} does less than any other correction tested. It
shifts the decision boundary from 0.5 to the class prior and nothing more
(Section~\ref{sec:methods-corrections}). Despite that, it is the most
robust method in the study on discrimination (Table~\ref{tab:robustness},
Section~\ref{sec:results-rq1}). It has the lowest CHR and
the highest mean CG in both directions, and its calibration is identical
to its own baseline's by construction, since a threshold shift never
touches the underlying predicted probabilities
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion-calibration} returns to why that is not the
same as leading on calibration outright). It leads on break-even share
too (Table~\ref{tab:breakeven}, Section~\ref{sec:results-rq3}), so its
advantage is not confined to
CHR and mean CG alone. \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} sit at the opposite end (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}
again). Both rescale TabPFN's
predictions against a prior and a temperature term, and hold the highest
CHR of any method in \emph{both} directions.

This finding builds on prior work rather than standing alone. Elor and
Averbuch-Elor~\cite{elor2022smote} found that on clean, uncorrupted data, a
well-tuned classifier often gains nothing from balancing the training set,
and can lose performance from it. This study's contribution is to show
that the same pattern holds, and grows sharper, once label noise is added.
A correction is not just sometimes unnecessary. It can actively backfire,
and in this comparison the two most mathematically elaborate corrections,
DistPFN and DistPFN-T, backfire hardest on discrimination, while the
simplest correction backfires least. Much of that backfiring is
concentrated at the balanced control ratio $r{=}0.50$, though not all of
it, restricted to genuinely imbalanced training sets ($r{\neq}0.50$), the
DistPFN pair's Direction B Correction Harm Rate falls from 43.8\%/47.4\%
pooled to 29.8\%/37.8\%, still four to twelve times
\texttt{tabpfn\_threshold}'s 0.3\% there
(Section~\ref{sec:results-r050}). The ranking does not change at any
scope. A practitioner choosing a correction method for a TabPFN
deployment under uncertain label quality and optimizing for balanced
accuracy has, based on this study's results, no clear reason to prefer a
more elaborate posterior-rescaling method over a simple threshold shift.
That recommendation does not extend to every deployment, however, a point
Section~\ref{sec:discussion-calibration} returns to.

There may be a second, more structural reason threshold correction leads
here, not just a general case for simplicity. This study's
severe-imbalance conditions combine imbalance with a real train and test
prior mismatch, since the test partition keeps each dataset's natural
class ratio while training is deliberately pushed down to $r$
(Section~\ref{sec:methods-datasets}). Threshold correction is nothing but
a prior-shift adjustment, and DistPFN and DistPFN-T target the same
mismatch through a more elaborate mechanism. All three are therefore
being tested in exactly the deployment scenario their underlying idea is
built for, which may explain part of why prior-adjusting corrections
outperform resampling and cost-sensitive weighting generally, separate
from how well each one specifically tolerates label contamination on top
of that shift. This coupling is broad-based, seven datasets sit at or
below the two most severe tested ratios (Section~\ref{sec:methods-datasets}),
so this structural explanation applies to a substantial share of the
study. It does not explain why the two DistPFN variants
fail so much more often than plain threshold correction while addressing
a closely related problem. That gap is a matter of contamination
robustness specifically, not of whether prior adjustment is the right
tool for this design.

\subsection{Robustness Check: Isolating the Balanced Control Condition ($r{=}0.50$)}
\label{sec:results-r050}

Section~\ref{sec:results-rq1}'s headline numbers pool all four tested
imbalance ratios, including $r{=}0.50$, a perfectly balanced training set
before contamination. $r{=}0.50$ is not itself an imbalanced condition,
and Figure~\ref{fig:gain-heatmap} already shows \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn}'s
harm concentrating specifically there under Direction B. This subsection
isolates it directly, every contribution metric and statistical test from
Sections~\ref{sec:results-rq1}--\ref{sec:results-significance} is
recomputed twice more, once restricted to $r \in \{0.20, 0.10, 0.05\}$
(the genuinely imbalanced subset, hereafter $r{\neq}0.50$) and once
restricted to $r{=}0.50$ alone (the balanced control), using the
identical formulas and the same already-collected evaluations. No model
was retrained or re-scored.

Table~\ref{tab:robustness-r050} reports Correction Harm Rate and mean
Correction Gain on the $r{\neq}0.50$ subset, directly comparable to
Table~\ref{tab:robustness}. \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} is unaffected in
its standing, if anything strengthened, its Direction B CHR falls from
2.4\% to 0.3\%, and its mean CG rises in both directions. The two DistPFN
variants change substantially but not completely. Under Direction B,
pooled CHR is 43.8\%/47.4\%; restricted to $r{\neq}0.50$ it falls to
29.8\% (\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn}) and 37.8\% (\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t}),
while their mean CG rises from a negative $-0.004$/$-0.009$ pooled to a
positive 0.013/$-0.004$. Isolated to $r{=}0.50$ alone, the same two
methods fail 85.6\% and 76.0\% of the time under Direction B, with a
clearly negative mean gain ($-0.055$, $-0.023$), and rarely reach
break-even at that ratio (3.8\% of (dataset, $r{=}0.50$) pairs for both,
versus 46.2\%/47.4\% at $r{\neq}0.50$). Under Direction A the shift is
milder, CHR for the DistPFN pair moves from 35.1\%/33.9\% pooled to
34.9\%/35.9\% at $r{\neq}0.50$, essentially unchanged.
\texttt{tabpfn\_downsample}'s CHR under Direction A does not fall when
$r{=}0.50$ is excluded (20.4\% pooled vs.\ 18.6\% at $r{\neq}0.50$),
consistent with Section~\ref{sec:discussion-direction}'s finding that its
failures are not tied to any one imbalance ratio.

Break-even and Prior Sensitivity results show a related but not identical
pattern. At $r{=}0.50$, mean PSG for the DistPFN pair under Direction A
falls to $-0.058$/$-0.059$, substantially more negative than the pooled
figures in Section~\ref{sec:results-rq4}; restricted to $r{\neq}0.50$, PSG
for both instead turns slightly positive under Direction A ($+0.010$,
$+0.009$), meaning the oracle prior modestly helps once the balanced
control is excluded, though it remains negative for both methods under
Direction B ($-0.013$, $-0.009$) and for \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} in
both directions regardless of $r$. Every Friedman omnibus test remains
significant on both the $r{\neq}0.50$ and $r{=}0.50$-only subsets, at both

Taken together, the core claims survive this check intact, and one gets
stronger, no correction is uniformly safe, method identity significantly
affects balanced accuracy in every family and direction regardless of
$r{=}0.50$'s inclusion, and \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} remains the most
robust method throughout, more clearly so once the balanced control is
removed. What changes is the magnitude attributed to the DistPFN pair's
failure under Direction B specifically, a real share of it, but not the
majority, is a property of the balanced condition rather than of
imbalance severity, since the pair's Direction B CHR only falls by
roughly a third (43.8\%$\to$29.8\% and 47.4\%$\to$37.8\%) once $r{=}0.50$
is excluded, not to the low single digits.

\subsection{Direction-Dependent Failure}
\label{sec:results-rq2}

Failure rates differ substantially between the two directed corruption
mechanisms, and the direction of that difference is itself method-dependent
rather than uniform.
Five of the seven methods have a higher CHR under Direction A
(majority rows relabeled as minority) than under Direction B (minority rows
relabeled as majority). These are \texttt{xgb\_ros}, \texttt{xgb\_smote},
\texttt{xgb\_weighted}, \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample}, and
\texttt{tabpfn\_threshold}, the last two by a wide margin
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness}). The two DistPFN variants show the opposite
pattern, failing more often under Direction B than Direction A.
Figure~\ref{fig:chr-regret} shows CHR and mean regret side by side for
every method, split by direction.

\begin{figure*}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\linewidth]{figures/chr_regret.png}
\caption{Correction Harm Rate (left) and mean regret (right, $\max(0,-\text{CG})$)
by method and direction, over noisy stress cells only, pooled across all
26 datasets. \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} have
both the highest CHR and the highest mean regret under both directions,
most severely under Direction B. \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} has the
lowest of both in both directions.}
\label{fig:chr-regret}
\end{figure*}

Regret and CHR tell a consistent story: the DistPFN pair
carries both the highest CHR and the highest mean regret in both
directions. Under Direction A, \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t}'s
mean regret (0.0108) is the highest of any method, ahead of
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} (0.0059) and \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample} (0.0023),
even though \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample}'s CHR (20.4\%) sits between the
DistPFN pair's (33.9--35.1\%) and the XGBoost methods' (13.0--14.7\%).
Under Direction B the gap widens sharply, \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t}'s
regret (0.0292) and \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn}'s (0.0239) are an order of
magnitude above every other method, all of which sit at 0.0004 or below.
\texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} has the lowest regret of any method in both
directions (0.0001 and 0.0000), consistent with its lead on every other
measure in this study.

Part of this asymmetry traces back to the uncorrected baselines themselves.
Comparing balanced accuracy at $c{=}0$ against $c{=}0.30$ for
\texttt{xgb\_none}, Direction B degrades performance by 0.076 (0.741 to
0.665) versus 0.034 for Direction A (0.741 to 0.707), roughly 2.2 times as
much. \texttt{tabpfn\_none} shows the same asymmetry, 0.066 (0.765 to 0.699)
versus 0.032 (0.765 to 0.733), roughly 2.0 times as much.
Losing genuine minority rows (Direction B) is inherently more damaging to
an uncorrected model than diluting the minority class with mislabeled
majority rows (Direction A), which helps explain why a correction's
apparent robustness under one direction does not predict its robustness
under the other.

\subsection{Direction Asymmetry Undercuts a Single Robustness Score}
\label{sec:discussion-direction}

Five of seven methods fail more often under Direction A (majority rows
relabeled as minority) than Direction B (minority rows relabeled as
majority), while the two DistPFN variants show the opposite pattern
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness}, Section~\ref{sec:results-rq2}). This
asymmetry is exactly what the label-noise literature's class-conditional
and asymmetric-noise formalizations
predict in principle~\cite{natarajan2013noisylabels,scott2013asymmetricnoise},
but this study's results show it is not a minor theoretical caveat. A
method's CHR under one direction is a poor predictor of its CHR under the
other. \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample}'s CHR is 20.4\% under Direction A and
7.7\% under Direction B (Table~\ref{tab:robustness}), nearly a threefold difference in the same method
on the same datasets. A robustness claim validated under only one
corruption mechanism, or under the symmetric random-flip noise more
commonly studied, does not transfer to a directed, class-conditional error
of the kind this study injects, and in practice the true direction of
label error is rarely known in advance. This is the strongest argument in
this study for testing every correction under both directions rather than
one.

\subsection{Break-Even Contamination Rates}
\label{sec:results-rq3}

of the 104 (dataset, $r$) combinations (26 datasets $\times$ 4 ratios)
whose seed-averaged Correction
Gain never dropped to zero or below within the tested range, i.e., $c^*>0.30$.
\texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} leads here too, staying net-beneficial through
the full range in 85.6\% of Direction A conditions and 87.5\% of Direction B
conditions, the highest share of any method in both directions, extending
its lead from CHR and mean CG to this measure as well.
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} sit at the

\subsection{Prior Sensitivity}
\label{sec:results-rq4}

On average, the deployable, observed-prior variant slightly outperforms
the oracle diagnostic rather than trailing it. Table~\ref{tab:psg} reports
mean PSG (Equation~\ref{eq:psg}), both pooled across all noisy cells and
restricted to the highest tested contamination, $c{=}0.30$, alone. Pooled,
it is negative for all three prior-dependent
methods in both directions. Under Direction A it is $-0.012$ for
\texttt{tabpfn\_threshold}, $-0.007$ for \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn}, and
$-0.008$ for \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t}, with \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold}
the largest of the three in magnitude despite being the most robust method
on balanced accuracy. Under Direction B the gap is $-0.008$, $-0.020$, and

\subsection{The Corrupted Prior Is Not the Main Failure Mode}
\label{sec:discussion-prior}

The most surprising result of this study is that mean PSG is negative for
all three prior-dependent TabPFN methods in both directions
(Section~\ref{sec:results-rq4}). The true,
uncorrupted prior, something no real deployment could ever supply, does
not reliably outperform the corrupted, observed one. Under Direction A,
at high contamination, the oracle variant of all three prior-dependent
methods degrades by a comparable or larger amount than the deployable
one (Figure~\ref{fig:psg}). If the wrong prior were the primary cause of these methods'
decline, correcting it should have helped. It does not, at least not
under Direction B or, at high contamination, under Direction A either.
Restricting to the genuinely imbalanced subset ($r{\neq}0.50$) does flip
mean PSG positive for both DistPFN variants under Direction A
($+0.010$, $+0.009$), meaning the corrupted prior does contribute
something to their Direction A decline once the balanced control is
excluded, it is simply not the dominant effect the pooled, more strongly
negative figure ($-0.058$/$-0.059$ at $r{=}0.50$ alone) would suggest
(Section~\ref{sec:results-r050}). The claim that the corrupted prior is
not the primary failure mode is therefore strongest for Direction B and,
increasingly at high contamination, for \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} too,
and more qualified for the DistPFN pair under Direction A at moderate
contamination.

One structural reason follows directly from how these corrections work. \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold}, \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn}, and
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} all adjust TabPFN's output probabilities after
the fact, using the class prior~\cite{saerens2002adjusting,lee2026distpfn}.
None of them touch the training rows TabPFN actually reads as its
in-context prompt~\cite{hollmann2023tabpfn,hollmann2025tabpfnv2}.
Contamination happens before any correction is applied, so the mislabeled
rows stay in that prompt regardless of which prior a posterior-rescaling
method is given afterward. Swapping in the true prior fixes the summary
statistic the correction uses, but not the individual wrong examples
TabPFN has already been shown. This would explain why the oracle prior
helps so little, though confirming it directly would require an
experiment this study did not run, comparing a prior-only fix against a
fix that also removes the mislabeled rows from the context itself.

\subsection{Calibration Reliability}
\label{sec:results-reliability}

Figure~\ref{fig:reliability} tracks Brier score and expected calibration
error against noise level for every method. Every XGBoost correction
improves on \texttt{xgb\_none}'s Brier score and ECE at every noise level
tested, in both directions, with no exception. The TabPFN family shows a
different kind of split. \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample} is worse-calibrated
than the uncorrected \texttt{tabpfn\_none} baseline on both ECE and Brier
score at every noise level tested under Direction A, the only method in
the study with that pattern on either metric. \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold}
never exceeds its baseline's Brier score or ECE in either direction,
which follows directly from how it works, it only moves the decision
threshold, so the underlying predicted probabilities that Brier score and
ECE measure are identical to \texttt{tabpfn\_none}'s by construction.
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} do not merely
avoid backfiring on calibration, they are the two best-calibrated methods
of any tested, correction or baseline, at every noise level in both
directions, with no exception. This holds despite the same two methods
having the highest Correction Harm Rate in the study on balanced accuracy
(Section~\ref{sec:results-rq1}): a DistPFN correction that more
frequently underperforms its own uncorrected baseline on balanced
accuracy can still be assigning its predictions a well-calibrated
probability, discrimination and calibration are not the same failure
mode. \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} remains consistent with its standing as
the most robust method on balanced accuracy
(Sections~\ref{sec:results-rq1} and~\ref{sec:results-rq2}), but on raw
calibration it trails the two DistPFN variants throughout.

\subsection{Accuracy and Calibration Answer Different Questions}
\label{sec:discussion-calibration}

Every XGBoost correction improves calibration over its baseline at every
noise level tested, even the ones that sometimes hurt balanced accuracy
(Figure~\ref{fig:reliability}, Section~\ref{sec:results-reliability}).
Under Direction A, \texttt{tabpfn\_downsample} is worse-calibrated than
doing nothing on both expected calibration error and Brier score at every
noise level tested. \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} take the opposite path entirely. The same two
methods with the highest Correction Harm Rate in the whole study
(Table~\ref{tab:robustness}) are also
its two best-calibrated methods, correction or baseline, at every
noise level in both directions. A method that more often underperforms
its own uncorrected baseline on discrimination can still be assigning its
predictions a well-calibrated probability. Balanced accuracy and
calibration measure different
properties of a classifier, and this study's results show a correction
can excel at one while failing badly at the other, not merely land
somewhere middling on both. This matters for deployment. A system that
only needs a correct label, such as most classification benchmarks,
cares primarily about balanced accuracy or a related discrimination
metric, and by that standard DistPFN and DistPFN-T are a poor choice
under contamination. A system that acts on a predicted probability
(setting a risk threshold, allocating a limited budget, ranking cases by
urgency) cares about calibration first, and for that use case their poor
CHR would be the wrong reason to rule them out. Reporting CHR and mean CG
alone for the headline comparisons would also miss that
\texttt{tabpfn\_downsample} is uniquely bad at calibration under
Direction A even though its balanced accuracy profile looks far more
favorable there, the second-highest mean CG of any method. Evaluating a
correction method for deployment requires checking both axes separately,
not inferring one from the other, and not assuming a method's rank on one
predicts its rank on the other.

\subsection{Implications for Imbalance Correction Under Label Noise}
\label{sec:discussion-implications}

Related work on class imbalance and label noise as a joint problem has so
far proposed new training-time algorithms, combining cleaning with
resampling~\cite{koziarski2020ccr}, active learning with noisy-label
training~\cite{khanal2024activelabel}, class-balanced sample
selection~\cite{liu2024imbalancednoisy}, or, inverting the relationship
entirely, deliberately engineered label noise designed to fix a known
class bias~\cite{hu2025beneficialnoise}. This study starts from a
different question. It asks whether the corrections practitioners are
already using need replacing at all. The answer depends on which
correction is already in use. \texttt{tabpfn\_threshold} stays
net-beneficial through the full contamination range in 85.6\% to 87.5\% of
the conditions tested (Table~\ref{tab:breakeven}), the highest share of any method in both
directions, so switching to it may fix most of what a new algorithm
would otherwise need to solve.
\texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn} and \texttt{tabpfn\_distpfn\_t} fail badly
enough on discrimination under high contamination, in both directions on
this study's full dataset pool, that the case for
joint noise-and-imbalance-aware training remains strong, for a
deployment that needs a correct label. A deployment that instead needs a
well-calibrated probability is a different case entirely
(Section~\ref{sec:discussion-calibration}), and swapping either DistPFN
variant out for a different correction there could trade away its best
property to fix its worst one. Telling these situations apart requires
the kind of audit this study performs, not an assumption that a
published correction is safe by default, or unsafe by default, on every
axis a deployment might care about.
