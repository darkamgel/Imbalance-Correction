\begin{abstract}
Imbalance-correction methods (resampling, cost-sensitive reweighting, and, for
tabular foundation models, test-time posterior rescaling) are usually assumed to be
safe. They might not always help much, but they are not supposed to hurt. That
assumption is almost never tested under label noise. It is validated on clean data,
then deployed into systems where labels are not clean. We test it directly, using
TabPFN~\cite{hollmann2023tabpfn,hollmann2025tabpfnv2} (threshold correction,
DistPFN~\cite{lee2026distpfn}, DistPFN-T, class-balanced downsampling) and
XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} (cost-sensitive weighting, random oversampling,
SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002smote}), we inject two directed, asymmetric label-error
mechanisms (majority rows mislabeled as minority, and minority rows lost into the
majority class) across 26 UCI datasets, at four imbalance ratios and five
contamination rates, holding total training-set size fixed so that changes in
class ratio are not confounded by changes in the amount of training data.
Across 112{,}319 successfully completed
model-condition evaluations (one additional evaluation failed to converge
numerically and is reported separately), all seven corrections underperformed the
uncorrected baseline in at least some tested conditions, with correction-harm rates
ranging from 2.4\% to 47.4\%, depending on the method and corruption direction. A
robustness check isolating the balanced ($r{=}0.50$) control condition from the
genuinely imbalanced ratios shows the upper end of that range falls to 37.8\% once
restricted to actual imbalance, while the most robust method's advantage is
unaffected or strengthened. Under some
dataset-imbalance settings, corrections first became non-beneficial at only 5\%
contamination. Correction-method performance differed significantly within both
model families under both corruption directions in the conservative, dataset-level
Friedman analysis (all four omnibus tests, $p < 10^{-9}$). No correction remained non-degrading across all tested conditions in
both corruption directions. If label quality cannot be guaranteed, and in practice it
rarely can be, imbalance correction should not be applied on the assumption that it
is harmless.
\end{abstract}
