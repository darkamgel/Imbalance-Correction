\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Tabular classifiers built for credit risk assessment, medical diagnosis, and spam
filtering routinely face two problems at once: the outcome of interest is rare, and
some of the labels describing that outcome are wrong. A rare disease is
underrepresented in a training set, and some of the recorded diagnoses in that same
set are themselves incorrect, because a reviewer missed a case, an automated pipeline
mislabeled a record, or an annotator simply disagreed with the correct answer. Three
of the five datasets used in this paper (credit risk, breast cancer diagnosis, and
spam email) are direct instances of this kind of task, and none of them come with a
guarantee that their labels are error free.

Practitioners already have a large toolbox for the first problem, class imbalance.
Resampling techniques such as SMOTE~\cite{chawla2002smote} and random oversampling,
cost-sensitive reweighting~\cite{elkan2001costsensitive}, and, for newer in-context
tabular models such as TabPFN~\cite{hollmann2023tabpfn,hollmann2025tabpfnv2},
test-time posterior rescaling methods such as DistPFN~\cite{lee2026distpfn} are all
designed to stop a model from ignoring a rare class. Every one of these methods is
validated, almost without exception, on data where the labels are assumed to be
correct. Whether they remain beneficial once the second problem, label noise, is
present at the same time is a question the existing literature has not directly
answered (Section~\ref{sec:related-work}).

This gap matters because the two problems are not independent once a correction is
applied. A resampling method decides how many synthetic or duplicated minority rows
to add based on the observed count of minority-labeled rows. A cost-sensitive method
sets its penalty in proportion to the observed class ratio. A posterior-rescaling
method for an in-context model corrects a predicted probability against the observed
minority rate. In every case, the correction is calibrated against an observed
quantity, not the true one. If a meaningful fraction of the rows behind that observed
quantity are mislabeled, in either direction, the correction inherits that error
without any way of detecting it. This paper is organized around four research
questions.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{RQ1.} Do standard imbalance corrections remain beneficial once
directed label error is present?
\item \textbf{RQ2.} Does the direction of label corruption change how often a
correction fails?
\item \textbf{RQ3.} At what contamination rate does each correction stop paying off?
\item \textbf{RQ4.} How much of a correction's failure is due to an incorrect prior
alone, rather than the corruption itself?
\end{itemize}

RQ1 and RQ2 are answered by Correction Gain and Correction Harm Rate, RQ3 by the
break-even contamination rate, and RQ4 by the Prior Sensitivity Gap, all defined in
Section~\ref{sec:methods}.

We test it directly. We construct two directed, asymmetric label-error mechanisms:
majority-class rows relabeled as minority (contamination), and minority-class rows
relabeled as majority (loss), and apply each at five contamination rates, including a
clean, uncorrupted baseline, to five real datasets drawn from the UCI Machine
Learning Repository~\cite{kelly_uci}. We test each corruption condition at four
imbalance ratios, and we hold the total training-set size fixed across those ratios
so that imbalance and label noise can be studied as independent factors rather than
confounded with how much training data is available. On top of this controlled
corruption we run seven correction methods spanning two model families in wide use,
TabPFN (threshold correction, DistPFN, DistPFN-T, class-balanced downsampling) and
XGBoost~\cite{chen2016xgboost} (cost-sensitive weighting, random oversampling,
SMOTE), across three random seeds, producing 6{,}417 individually verified
model-condition evaluations.

To judge whether a correction helps or hurts under this design, we use four
purpose-built metrics, defined in full in Section~\ref{sec:methods}. Correction Gain
is the difference in performance between a corrected method and its uncorrected
baseline at the same imbalance and noise condition. Correction Harm Rate is the
fraction of tested conditions where that gain is negative, that is, where applying
the correction leaves the model worse off than doing nothing at all. The break-even
contamination rate is the noise level at which a method stops paying off. The Prior
Sensitivity Gap is how much better a correction would perform if it were given the
true, uncorrupted class prior instead of the observed one, isolating how much of a
correction's failure comes specifically from trusting a corrupted prior.

Across the 6{,}417 evaluations, every one of the seven correction methods we test has
a nonzero Correction Harm Rate. Depending on the method and the direction of
corruption, the harm rate ranges from 0.6\% to 31.7\% of tested conditions, and for
some method-dataset pairs the break-even contamination rate is as low as 5\%. These
differences are statistically significant in every model family and corruption
direction we test (Friedman, $p < 10^{-11}$ in all four tests). No single method is
robust to both corruption directions, and which method is most robust depends on the
dataset it is applied to.

Our contributions are as follows.

\begin{itemize}
\item We introduce a controlled experimental design that holds total training-set
size fixed across imbalance ratios and injects two directed, asymmetric label-error
mechanisms on top of it, so that imbalance and label noise can be varied as
independent factors rather than as a single confounded variable.
\item We audit seven correction methods across two model families in practical use,
including DistPFN and DistPFN-T, whose reference implementation we check directly
against the authors' published code before using it in this study, and we report an
implementation error we found in that process (Section~\ref{sec:methods}).
\item We define four contribution metrics, Correction Gain, Correction Harm Rate,
break-even contamination rate, and Prior Sensitivity Gap, built specifically to
measure whether a correction remains net-beneficial once label noise is present,
rather than measuring model accuracy alone.
\item We show, across five real datasets and 6{,}417 evaluated conditions, that every
correction method tested can underperform doing nothing at all, at contamination
rates as low as 5\%, and that this failure is direction-dependent rather than
uniform across methods or datasets.
\end{itemize}
