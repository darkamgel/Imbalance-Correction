\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper set out to test whether standard imbalance corrections
remain worth applying once the labels they depend on cannot be
trusted. Across five datasets, seven correction methods spanning two
model families, and two directed label-error mechanisms, every
correction audited underperformed its matched uncorrected baseline in
at least some tested condition. How often and how badly it does so,
however, is neither uniform nor predictable from a single number. It
depends on the method, on the direction of the label error, and on
whether balanced accuracy or calibration is the metric being judged.
The simplest correction tested, a threshold shift keyed to the class
prior, was consistently the most robust across every measure but one.
The two most mathematically involved corrections, DistPFN and
DistPFN-T, were consistently among the least, the opposite of what
their added sophistication might suggest.

The four contribution metrics introduced here, Correction Gain,
Correction Harm Rate, Regret, and the break-even contamination rate,
made this possible by separating whether a correction helps from how
often and how badly it fails when it does not. The same framework
surfaced a result this paper did not anticipate. The corrupted prior
is not the primary reason TabPFN's posterior-rescaling corrections
decline under contamination, since handing them the true prior did
not reliably help, and on average even hurt slightly.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion-prior} offers one explanation, that these
corrections never touch the mislabeled rows still sitting in TabPFN's
in-context prompt, but confirming it would need an experiment this
paper did not run, one that cleans the context directly rather than
only adjusting the prior applied to it. That experiment, together with
testing whether the same robustness ranking holds on larger datasets,
under instance-dependent noise, and on tabular foundation models
beyond TabPFN, is the most direct way to build on what is reported
here.
