\section{Conclusion and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper asked whether standard imbalance corrections remain worth applying
once the labels they depend on cannot be trusted. Across 26 datasets, seven
correction methods in two model families, and two directed label-error
mechanisms, every correction underperformed its matched uncorrected baseline in
at least some tested condition. How often and how severely this occurred
depended on the method, the direction of label error, and whether performance
was judged by balanced accuracy or calibration. No single metric fully
characterizes this behavior.

The simplest correction, a threshold shift based on the class prior, was the
most robust in terms of discrimination, leading every other method on
Correction Harm Rate and mean Correction Gain in both directions. The DistPFN
and DistPFN-T corrections had the highest Correction Harm Rate under both
directions, exceeding the next-highest method's rate by more than a factor of
five under Direction B. Greater correction complexity therefore did not
translate into greater robustness. At the same time, these methods produced the
best-calibrated probabilities in the study. Excluding the balanced $r{=}0.50$
control shows that part of their discrimination loss is concentrated there: the
DistPFN pair's Direction B Correction Harm Rate falls from 43.8\%/47.4\% to
29.8\%/37.8\%, while the threshold method's advantage holds or strengthens. A
correction can therefore degrade discrimination relative to its baseline while
still improving probability calibration. The results surface this split without
resolving it in either method's favor.

The five proposed metrics provide a structured way to characterize these
effects. They distinguish the magnitude of a correction's benefit from how
often it becomes harmful, how severe that harm is, and how much of the observed
degradation can be attributed to corruption of the class prior. Applying these
metrics also revealed an unexpected result: corruption of the prior does not
appear to be the primary cause of the degradation observed in TabPFN's
posterior-rescaling corrections. Providing these methods with the true
pre-corruption prior did not reliably improve performance and, on average,
produced a small additional degradation. Section~\ref{sec:discussion-prior}
offers one explanation: these corrections modify the prior but do not alter the
mislabeled examples that remain in TabPFN's in-context prompt.

Several directions follow from these findings. Confirming this explanation
would require removing or correcting mislabeled examples in the in-context
prompt directly rather than only adjusting the prior applied to it. A
complementary matched-prior regime, in which the test partition is resampled to
each tested $r$, would help separate robustness to class imbalance from
robustness to the train/test prior mismatch induced by the present design. Such
an experiment would complement the deployment-oriented evaluation used here by
isolating these two effects.

Further work should also test whether the observed robustness ranking persists
when substantially larger controlled training sets are available and when
tabular foundation models other than TabPFN are used. Extending the evaluation
to multiclass imbalance, instance-dependent label noise, and corruption acting
simultaneously in both directions would further establish the generality of the
findings.
